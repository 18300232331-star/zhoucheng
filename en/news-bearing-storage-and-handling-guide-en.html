Bearing Storage and Handling: Keep Bearings Clean and Ready to Install

Published: 2026-11-07 　|　 Industry News

Store bearings in their original packing, in a clean, dry place, and handle them with clean hands and tools. A bearing that is stored damp, dropped, or opened months before fitting can fail early even if it was made perfectly — most storage damage is invisible until the bearing is running.

1. Keep the packing on until fitting day

The packing a bearing arrives in is part of the product, not waste to throw away on arrival. It keeps moisture, dust and knocks away from surfaces that are finished to very tight tolerances.

  • Do not open a bearing early. A bearing opened weeks before fitting sits exposed to dust and humidity for no reason. Open it only when the fitting is about to happen, and fit it the same day it is opened where possible.
  • Keep the packing intact. If the outer box is torn or the inner wrapping is damaged in transit, note it on receipt and inspect the bearing before it goes into stock. A damaged wrapping may mean the bearing inside has already been exposed.
  • Keep the designation visible. The packing carries the designation that identifies the bearing — bore, series and suffixes. Keep the label with the bearing until it is fitted, so the right bearing goes to the right job and nothing is guessed from memory.
  • Do not rewrap in anything that sheds fibres. Cloths, paper and rags that leave fibres behind are worse than no wrapping at all. If a bearing must be rewrapped, use a clean, lint-free material and keep the designation label with it.

A bearing is a precision part: the surfaces that matter are finished smooth enough that a speck of dust or a fingerprint can start damage. The packing is the cheapest protection it will ever have.

2. Where to store: clean, dry, still and off the floor

Storage is mostly about what the bearing is kept away from: moisture, dust, vibration and knocks.

  • Clean and dry first. The store should be a clean room or a clean shelf, not a corner of a workshop where grinding dust and cutting fluid are in the air. Moisture is the main enemy: damp air rusts a bearing surface faster than most buyers expect, and rust on a raceway is damage, not dirt.
  • Stable temperature. Avoid places that swing between hot and cold, which draw condensation onto steel. A steady indoor temperature matters more than a cool one.
  • Off the floor and off the ground. Store bearings on shelves or pallets, never directly on a concrete floor, where damp rises and where they are kicked, flooded or run over. Small bearings belong in drawers or bins; larger ones on a shelf with nothing stacked on top of them.
  • Away from vibration. Do not store bearings next to running machines or compressors. Vibration on a stationary bearing can mark the raceways over time — the bearing looks untouched, but the damage is already there.
  • Away from chemicals and magnets. Keep bearings away from solvents, acids, cleaning chemicals and strong magnetic fields, all of which can affect the steel or the packing grease.

If the store is shared with other parts, give bearings their own shelf or cabinet. A bearing stored loose in a bin of mixed parts will be scratched, dented or mixed up with the wrong designation — and a mixed-up bearing is a wrong bearing fitted by mistake.

3. Handling rules: clean hands, clean tools, no drops

Most handling damage happens in the minute between the shelf and the machine. These rules cover that minute.

  • Wash and dry your hands first. Skin moisture and workshop dirt transfer straight onto the bearing. Handle bearings with clean, dry hands, and wear clean gloves where the work allows them. Never handle a bearing with oily, gritty or wet hands.
  • Use clean tools and a clean bench. The bench, the puller, the sleeve and the rags should be clean before the bearing comes out of its packing. A clean bearing fitted with a dirty tool is a dirty bearing.
  • Never drop a bearing, and never spin a dry one. A drop can dent a raceway or crack a ring even when the bearing looks fine afterwards — if a bearing is dropped, set it aside and inspect it, and ask the supplier if you are unsure whether it is still fit to use. Spinning a dry bearing by hand before fitting wears the surfaces without lubricant, so do not spin it to "check" it.
  • Lift by the right ring, not by the cage. Carry and position the bearing by the ring that will be fitted, and do not press on the cage or the seals. Force through the wrong part is how a new bearing is damaged before it ever turns.
  • Keep the bore protected. Do not put a bearing down on a dirty surface bore-first, and do not slide it across a bench. The bore is a finished surface: a scratch there is a fitting problem later.

4. Storage time, rotation and the check before fitting

Bearings do not improve in storage, so the stock should turn over rather than sit.

  • First in, first out. Use the oldest stock first, and keep the packing labels visible so the rotation is easy to follow. New deliveries go behind the old stock, not in front of it.
  • Check the packing before use. Before fitting a bearing that has been in stock for a long time, look at the packing first: is it intact, dry and unmarked? If the packing is damaged, damp or torn, inspect the bearing itself before fitting — look for rust, marks or dents, and confirm with the supplier if anything is unclear.
  • Keep stock records by designation. A simple list — designation, quantity, date received — stops two common mistakes: fitting the wrong bearing because two look alike, and ordering more of a bearing that is already on the shelf.
  • Ask about shelf life for your conditions. How long a bearing can be stored depends on the packing, the grease and the store itself, so there is no single number that fits every buyer. Confirm the storage guidance for the bearings you order at inquiry, and tell the supplier how long you expect to hold stock.

Bearings for small and medium applications, up to 600 mm outside diameter, are the range this guide covers. Storage practice is the same across the range, but the heavier the bearing, the more a drop or a knock matters — handle large pieces with the lifting help they need, not by hand.

5. Before installation: a short checklist

Fitting is a separate job with its own guide, but the storage handover is short:

  • The bearing is the designation ordered, checked against the packing label
  • The packing was intact until fitting day
  • The bearing shows no rust, dents, marks or damaged seals
  • Hands, tools and bench are clean and dry
  • The shaft and housing are clean, and the fitting method matches the bearing type

If any item on that list fails, stop and sort it out before fitting. A bearing fitted dirty, damaged or wrong is a bearing that will fail early, and the failure will be blamed on the bearing rather than on the fitting.

Frequently asked questions

How long can I store a bearing before fitting it? There is no single number — it depends on the packing, the grease and the store. Keep the packing intact, the store clean and dry, and rotate stock first in, first out. Confirm the storage guidance for your order at inquiry.

Is a little surface rust on a stored bearing a problem? Yes. Rust on a raceway or rolling element is damage, not dirt, and it does not wash off into a good bearing again. If you find rust, do not fit the bearing — ask the supplier first.

Can I wash a bearing that got dirty in storage? Do not wash it in a solvent and fit it anyway. Cleaning removes the protective grease and can push dirt further in. Set the bearing aside and ask the supplier how to proceed.

Does it matter if the packing is torn but the bearing looks fine? Yes. A torn packing means the bearing has been exposed to whatever was in the air. Inspect it before fitting, and do not fit it if you find marks, rust or dents.

Can I store different bearing types on the same shelf? Yes, if each designation is kept separate and labelled, and nothing is stacked on top of a bearing. Mixed, unlabelled storage is how the wrong bearing gets fitted.
