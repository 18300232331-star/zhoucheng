How to Choose a Bearing by Load, Speed and Application

Published: 2026-11-04 　|　 Industry News

Choosing the wrong bearing rarely shows up on the day a machine is built. It shows up later, as heat, noise, vibration, or a shaft that stops when it should keep running. Learning how to choose a bearing means answering four questions in order: what load does it carry, how fast does it turn, what conditions does it work in, and how much space and accuracy does the machine allow? This guide covers small and medium bearings, with an outer diameter up to 600 mm.

Start with the load direction

Load direction is the fastest way to narrow the choice. A radial load acts at right angles to the shaft, as in a motor shaft, a fan shaft, or a conveyor roller. An axial load, also called a thrust load, acts along the shaft, as in a screw drive or a gearbox that pushes the shaft sideways under load. Many real machines combine the two.

If the load is mainly radial, with only a small axial load in either direction, a ball bearing is often the natural starting point. Deep groove ball bearings are widely used for exactly this duty: they are simple, run with low friction, and suit light and medium loads at higher speeds. If the axial load is large and acts in one direction, or if a pair of bearings must take axial load in both directions, a tapered design deserves a closer look. Tapered roller bearings carry combined radial and one-direction axial loads, and mounted as a pair they handle axial load in both directions with good rigidity.

A common mistake is to choose by size alone, ignoring direction. A bearing that is strong radially can still fail early if it is repeatedly pushed sideways beyond what its design is meant to take. Before comparing any two types, write down the load direction, and whether the axial load reverses.

Then judge the size and character of the load

Next, judge how heavy the load is, and whether it is steady, varying, or shock-like. Steady loads from a balanced motor or a smooth-running pump are kinder to a bearing than the repeated impacts found in pressing or rough construction duty. Shock and vibration call for a more robust roller design and a careful check of the shaft and housing support, not simply a larger bearing of the same type.

For heavy radial loads where rigidity matters, line contact has a clear advantage over point contact. Cylindrical roller bearings spread a radial load along the length of each roller, giving high radial capacity and stiffness for gearboxes, spindles, and pumps. That same design, however, is not the right answer for a large axial load on its own, so the load direction from the first step still governs.

Speed changes the answer

Speed is the third filter, and it often overturns a choice made on load alone. Ball bearings generally suit higher rotational speeds with lower friction and less heat. Roller bearings suit heavier loads, but at very high speeds their friction, heat, and lubrication needs must be checked more carefully. A bearing that is adequate at a moderate speed can overheat if the same machine is later run much faster without a change in lubrication or cooling.

Ask three practical speed questions. What is the normal running speed, not just the maximum speed on the nameplate? Does the machine start and stop often, or run continuously? Frequent starts can squeeze lubricant out of the contact area and make the first seconds of each run harder on the bearing. Is there room for heat to escape, or is the bearing enclosed in a hot housing? If heat cannot escape, the safe speed for a given design falls, and the lubricant choice becomes more important.

For general background, see bearing types and load directions. The final selection should still be confirmed against the size, load case, and working conditions in the inquiry.

Match the bearing to the application

The same bearing type can behave very differently in a clean electric motor, a dusty agricultural machine, a wet plant, or a hot gearbox. Contamination is a common cause of early failure, so sealing and lubrication must be chosen for the place where the bearing will actually work, not for a clean test bench.

In dusty or damp surroundings, a sealed or shielded execution protects the rolling elements and keeps lubricant in place. In a clean, enclosed gearbox with its own oil supply, an open bearing with oil lubrication may be the better and simpler choice. Where washdown, steam, or fine dust is present, say so plainly in the inquiry, because that single detail can change the sealing recommendation.

Misalignment is another factor buyers often overlook. Long shafts and flexible housings can tilt one ring relative to the other, and some designs tolerate this better than others. Flag a long shaft or hard-to-hold alignment early. Temperature matters too: heat thins lubricant and changes clearance, while cold can stiffen grease.

Size, fit, clearance, and precision

With type, load, and speed settled, the remaining details decide whether a good choice lasts. First, confirm bore diameter, outside diameter, and width. In this small and medium range, outside diameter runs up to 600 mm, so a size that fits the shaft and housing without forcing or rework is the first practical test.

Second, think about fit and clearance together. A fit that is too loose allows creep and wear; a fit that is too tight, combined with heat, can remove the running clearance the bearing needs. If the machine runs hot, uses a tight fit, or carries a heavy load, ask for a clearance recommendation for that exact combination instead of assuming a standard clearance suits every case.

Third, choose the precision class for the job, not for prestige. Most general machinery runs well on a standard precision class. Spindles, high-speed motors, and instruments needing low vibration and tight running accuracy may need a higher class, but higher precision helps only when the shaft, housing, and assembly accuracy can match it.

Finally, settle lubrication at the selection stage. Grease suits many sealed, maintenance-free applications; oil suits higher speeds, hotter duty, and systems where oil is already circulated and filtered.

A short selection checklist

Use this checklist before sending an inquiry, to avoid delay while a supplier asks for basic information the buyer already knows.

  1. Load direction: radial only, axial only, or combined? Does the axial load reverse?
  2. Load character: steady, varying, or shock-like? Heaviest at start-up, at full speed, or under impact?
  3. Speed: normal running speed, maximum speed, and whether the machine starts and stops often.
  4. Environment: dust, moisture, washdown, chemicals, or high temperature?
  5. Alignment: short and rigid shaft, or long and likely to deflect?
  6. Dimensions: bore, outside diameter (up to 600 mm in this range), and width, plus any housing limit.
  7. Sealing and lubrication: sealed, shielded, or open? Grease or oil?
  8. Precision and clearance: standard duty, or low vibration and high running accuracy?
  9. Quantity and timing: how many are needed, and is this a trial, a repeat order, or a custom size?

Send the bearing number if known, or the dimensions and answers above if not. A drawing or sample helps where a standard size does not fit. Contact us for pricing and MOQ, and confirm lead time and packing in writing before any payment.

Mistakes to avoid

The most frequent mistake is to replace a failed bearing with a larger bearing of the same type without asking why the first one failed. If the cause was contamination, poor lubrication, misalignment, or an axial load the type was never meant to carry, a larger bearing of the same type will fail for the same reason. Fix the cause, then choose the type.

The second mistake is to chase the lowest friction or the highest speed rating while ignoring load and environment. The best choice balances load capacity, speed, sealing, and lubrication for the real machine. The third mistake is to leave sealing and lubrication until the end, by which time the housing may already suit a different execution.

Frequently asked questions

Can one bearing type suit both high speed and heavy load? Sometimes, but there is always a trade-off. Ball designs favour speed and low friction; roller designs favour heavy radial load and rigidity. Where both matter, speed, load, and lubrication must be checked together.

What if no standard size fits, or I have no bearing number? Send the dimensions, load case, and working conditions, or simply work through the checklist above, and ask for availability and pricing to be confirmed in writing.

Choosing a bearing well means: direction first, then load character, then speed, then application and environment, and finally fit, clearance, precision, and lubrication. Follow that order, stay inside the small and medium range up to 600 mm outside diameter, and send a complete inquiry.
