Deep Groove Ball Bearing Sizes: How to Read a Size Chart and Order the Right Bearing

Published: 2026-11-14 　|　 Industry News

A size chart lists every bearing by three numbers: the bore (the hole in the middle), the outside diameter (the outer ring), and the width. Find your bore in the left column, read across to the outside diameter and the width, and you have the size to order — the rest of the chart tells you the series, the seal type and the version. Read the chart in that order and the wrong size never gets ordered.

1. The three numbers that define a size

Every bearing size is three measurements, and the chart is built around them. The bore is the inside diameter — the hole that fits on the shaft. The outside diameter is the outer ring — the circle that fits in the housing. The width is the thickness across the bearing, measured from one side face to the other. Those three numbers, in millimetres, are the size. Everything else on the chart — the series, the seal, the version — describes the same size in more detail.

Start with the bore, because the shaft decides it. The shaft diameter is fixed by the machine, so the bore is the one number you usually cannot change. Find the bore in the chart's left column first, and only then look at the outside diameter and the width. A buyer who starts from the outside diameter often finds a bearing that fits the housing but not the shaft — and a bearing that does not fit the shaft is the wrong bearing, however good the rest of the row looks.

If you have the old bearing in your hand, measure it instead of guessing from the chart: our guide to measuring a bearing explains how to take the three numbers with a caliper. The chart is for ordering, when you know the machine and need the size; the caliper is for identifying, when you have the part and need its numbers. Use the right tool for the job you actually have.

2. How a size chart is laid out

A deep groove ball bearing size chart is a table, and every table works the same way. The rows are grouped by bore size, smallest first. Inside each bore group there are several rows — one for each series — and each row gives the outside diameter and the width for that series. Read down the bore column to your shaft size, then read across the row to the outside diameter and the width.

Take a standard example from the chart. The size known as 6204 has a bore of 20 mm, an outside diameter of 47 mm and a width of 14 mm. The digits are not random: the last two digits give the bore (04 means 20 mm), the digit before them gives the series, and the first digit gives the bearing type. That is how one row of the chart is read — bore first, then outside diameter, then width, with the series sitting in the middle of the code. Our guide to reading a bearing number explains the code in full; the chart itself only needs the three numbers.

Watch for two traps. First, the same bore appears in several series, and each series has a different outside diameter and width — so the bore alone is never the size. Second, widths differ between series even when the bore and the outside diameter match, so check all three numbers on the row, not just the first two. A row read halfway is a size ordered wrong.

3. Series: why the same bore comes in different sizes

The series is the middle of the size code, and it decides how heavy the bearing is for its bore. A light series has a smaller outside diameter and a narrower width; a heavier series has a larger outside diameter and a wider width for the same bore. The bore stays the same — the shaft does not change — but the bearing around it gets bigger.

Choose the series by the space and the job, not by habit. If the housing is small and the load is light, the light series fits and the heavier one does not. If the load is heavier, the heavier series is the one to ask about — but confirm the housing space first, because a heavier series needs a bigger housing, and a housing that cannot take it means a redesign, not a reorder. The chart shows all the series for each bore side by side, so the trade is visible in one look: same bore, different outside diameter, different width.

Do not order a heavier series "to be safe" without checking the housing. A bearing that does not fit the housing is scrap, and a heavier series ordered for a light job is money spent on steel the machine never needed. Send the housing dimensions with the inquiry and let the supplier confirm the series — that confirmation is part of the order, not an extra.

4. Seals, shields and versions: the rest of the row

The size is three numbers, but the row carries more. The seal type decides whether the bearing is open, shielded or sealed — open for clean, oiled housings, shielded or sealed where dust and dirt are present. The version covers details such as the cage and the clearance, and the chart marks them with letters after the size code. Read those letters before ordering: an open bearing and a sealed bearing with the same three numbers are different parts, and they do not swap.

Check the seal against the working place. A bearing in a dusty workshop, on a farm machine or near a grinding line needs protection from dirt; a bearing inside a clean gearbox with its own oil supply may not. If you are not sure which version the machine needs, say so in the inquiry and describe the working place — dust, water, temperature, how often the machine runs. The supplier confirms the version from that description.

5. Ordering from the chart: what to send with the inquiry

An inquiry from a size chart needs five things: the three numbers (bore, outside diameter, width), the series, the seal or shield type, and the quantity. Add the application — what machine it goes in and what it does — so the supplier can confirm the series and the version before quoting. A size without an application can be quoted; a size with an application can be checked, and the check is what stops a wrong order.

The minimum order is 50 pieces. We supply small and medium bearings, with outside diameters up to 600 mm; larger sizes are outside our range, so check the outside diameter against that limit before sending the inquiry. Dimensions, quantities beyond the minimum, prices and delivery are confirmed at inquiry — send the three numbers and the application, and the supplier confirms the rest.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three numbers in a bearing size? The bore (the hole in the middle), the outside diameter (the outer ring) and the width. Those three numbers, in millimetres, are the size. Find the bore first, then read the outside diameter and the width across the same row.

Can two bearings have the same bore but different sizes? Yes. The same bore comes in several series, and each series has a different outside diameter and width. The bore alone is never the size — always check all three numbers on the row.

How do I know which series to order? By the housing space and the job. A light series fits a small housing and a light load; a heavier series needs a bigger housing. Send the housing dimensions with the inquiry and let the supplier confirm the series.

What is the difference between open, shielded and sealed bearings? It is the protection against dirt. Open bearings suit clean, oiled housings; shielded or sealed bearings suit dusty or dirty working places. The seal type is marked by letters after the size code — read them before ordering.

What should I send with a bearing inquiry? The three numbers, the series, the seal or shield type, the quantity, and the application — what machine it goes in and what it does. The minimum order is 50 pieces, and outside diameters go up to 600 mm.
