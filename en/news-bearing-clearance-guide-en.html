Bearing Clearance Explained: C2, CN, C3 and C4 — Which Clearance to Order

Published: 2026-11-08 　|　 Industry News

Bearing clearance is the small internal play between the rings and the rolling elements — the tiny amount the bearing can move before it is fitted and loaded. CN is the normal group; C2 is tighter than normal, C3 is looser, and C4 is looser still. Most general machinery runs on CN, and you only move to another group when heat, speed or a tight fit gives you a reason.

1. What clearance is, and why it is not a defect

A new bearing is not a solid block. Between the inner ring, the rolling elements and the outer ring there is a small designed gap, measured as radial internal clearance. That gap is not looseness and it is not a manufacturing error — it is there on purpose, and the size of the gap is chosen when the bearing is ordered.

Clearance matters because a bearing changes shape once it is working. Fitting squeezes it: pressing a bearing onto a shaft or into a housing takes up some of the play. Heat expands it: a bearing that runs hot grows, and the rings and the rolling elements do not all grow by the same amount. Load presses it: under radial load the play shifts to one side. If the clearance left after all of that is too small, the rolling elements bind, the bearing runs hot, and it fails early. If it is too large, the bearing runs noisy and rough, and the shaft is not held firmly.

So clearance is a choice, not a fixed property. The same bearing size can be ordered in different clearance groups, and the right group depends on the machine it goes into — which is why a complete inquiry states the clearance group, or states the running conditions so the supplier can confirm it.

2. The four groups: C2, CN, C3, C4

Clearance groups are named in the same standards this site already states for its bearings — GB/T 307 and ISO 492 — and the names are the same across makers, so a group asked for by name is understood anywhere.

  • C2 — tighter than normal. Less internal play than the normal group. It suits light loads, low speeds and cool running, where a tighter bearing gives smoother, quieter running and firmer shaft guidance. It is the wrong choice where heat or a tight fit will take up the little play it has.
  • CN — normal. The standard group, and the default when no group is stated. Most general machinery — motors, pumps, fans, gearboxes running at ordinary temperatures and speeds — runs on CN. If you do not have a specific reason to choose another group, CN is the order.
  • C3 — looser than normal. More internal play than CN. It is the common choice where the bearing runs hot, runs fast, or is fitted with a tight interference fit that takes up play. Many electric motors and machines that warm up in service are ordered C3 as a matter of routine.
  • C4 — looser still. More play again, for the hottest, fastest or tightest-fitted duties. It is a specialist choice rather than a default: it suits machines where heat growth and tight fits would otherwise leave no play at all.

The groups are ordered, not ranked: C4 is not "better" than CN, it is simply looser. A bearing with more clearance than its machine needs runs rough and noisy, just as a bearing with too little runs hot and binds. The right group is the one that leaves the correct working clearance once the bearing is fitted, warm and loaded.

3. What pushes the choice one way or the other

Three running conditions decide the group. Work through them for the machine in question, and the choice usually narrows to one group.

  • Heat. A bearing that runs hot expands, and the expansion takes up clearance. Hot running — a gearbox in a warm plant, a motor under steady load, a dryer-section roll — points toward a looser group, because the bearing needs play left over once it is warm. Cool, lightly loaded running points the other way.
  • Fit. A tight interference fit squeezes the bearing as it is pressed on. A shaft fit or housing fit that is tight takes up clearance before the bearing ever turns, so tight fits point toward a looser group. A loose or sliding fit leaves the clearance as made, so the normal group stays correct.
  • Speed and load. High speed generates heat, which points toward a looser group for the same reason as hot running. Heavy load presses the rolling elements and takes up play on the loaded side. Light load and low speed leave the clearance close to as made, so the normal group is usually right.

There is no shortcut past these three. Two machines with the same bearing size can need different groups because one runs hot and the other runs cool, or because one has a tight shaft fit and the other does not. That is why the inquiry should state the running conditions, not just the size.

4. The mistakes buyers make with clearance

Most clearance problems come from three habits, and all three are easy to avoid.

  • Ordering by habit. A buyer who always orders C3 because "that is what we always order" will put a loose bearing into a cool, light machine that needed CN — and the machine will run noisy and rough. Order the group for the machine in front of you, not for the last machine.
  • Leaving the group out of the inquiry. If no group is stated, the normal group CN is supplied. That is correct for most general machinery, but it is wrong for a hot-running or tight-fitted machine that needed C3. If the machine runs hot or the fit is tight, say so in the inquiry — do not leave the group to chance.
  • Confusing clearance with precision class. Clearance and precision are different things. Precision classes — P0, P6, P5 and the like, covered in the precision-classes article on this site — describe how accurately the bearing is made. Clearance describes how much internal play it has. A high-precision bearing can still be ordered in any clearance group, and a normal-precision bearing can be ordered loose or tight. State both, separately, in the inquiry.

5. What to send at inquiry

A clearance question is answered in one message, if the message carries the right facts.

  • The bearing designation, without guessing the clearance. Send the size and type you need. If you know the group from the old bearing — it is usually marked on the bearing or on the old order — send that too. If you do not know it, say so rather than guessing.
  • The running conditions. Speed, load, operating temperature, and whether the shaft and housing fits are tight. Three lines of running conditions are worth more than a guessed group.
  • The machine and the application. A motor, a pump, a gearbox, a fan — and what it drives. The application tells the supplier what the bearing lives through, and it is the fastest way to confirm the group.
  • The quantity and the delivery need. Clearance is confirmed per order, so state the quantity and when the bearings are needed, and confirm the group in writing before the order is placed.

General background on how a bearing works notes that the rolling elements carry the load between the rings, and the space between them is what lets the bearing turn freely. The lesson for clearance is the same as for the bearing itself: the right amount of play is a working condition, not a number to maximise — and the group that leaves the bearing free when it is fitted, warm and loaded is the group to order.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if I order the wrong clearance? Too little clearance left after fitting and warming up makes the bearing run hot and bind, and it fails early. Too much clearance makes it run noisy and rough and hold the shaft loosely. Either way the bearing does not last as it should — so the group is worth stating correctly at inquiry.

If I do not state a clearance group, what do I get? The normal group, CN. That is right for most general machinery at ordinary temperatures and speeds. If your machine runs hot, runs fast or has tight fits, state the conditions in the inquiry so the group can be confirmed rather than assumed.

Is C3 better than CN? No — it is looser, not better. C3 is the right choice where heat, speed or a tight fit would otherwise take up the play. In a cool, lightly loaded machine, CN is the right choice and C3 would leave the bearing too loose.

Can I read the clearance group off the old bearing? Usually yes — the group is marked on the bearing or on the old order, alongside the designation. If the marking is worn or unclear, send the size, type and running conditions instead of guessing the group.

What should I send with a clearance inquiry? The bearing size and type, the running speed, load and temperature, how tight the shaft and housing fits are, and the machine and application. If you know the old group, send it; if you do not, send the conditions and ask for the group to be confirmed.
