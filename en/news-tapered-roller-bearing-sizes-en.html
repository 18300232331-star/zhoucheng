Tapered Roller Bearing Sizes: How to Read the Size Chart and Order the Right Bearing

Published: 2026-11-16 　|　 Industry News

A tapered roller bearing is two parts that work as one: a cone that carries the rollers, and a cup that they roll in. Read its size chart the same way you read any bearing chart — bore, outside diameter and width — but read it knowing the shape is tapered, so the bearing handles push from the side as well as weight from above. Find your bore in the left column, read across to the outside diameter and the width, and you have the size to order; the rest of the chart tells you the series and the version.

1. What makes a tapered roller bearing different

A ball bearing rolls on points. A tapered roller bearing rolls on lines — the rollers are cones, set at an angle, running between a cone and a cup that match that angle. That angle is the whole point: it lets the bearing take a push along the shaft as well as weight across it, at the same time. A ball bearing asked to do the same job wears out early; a tapered roller bearing is built for it.

That is why you find tapered roller bearings where the load pushes and pulls at once: wheel hubs, gearboxes, axle boxes and machine tool spindles. If your machine only carries weight straight down, a deep groove ball bearing may be the simpler and cheaper answer. If the shaft is pushed from the side while it turns — and most working shafts are — the tapered roller is the bearing to look at first.

The bearing comes as a matched pair of parts: the cone (inner ring with rollers) and the cup (outer ring). They are made to fit each other, and they are ordered together. Do not order a cone without its cup, and do not mix a cone from one size with a cup from another — the pair is the bearing.

2. The three dimensions on the chart

Every size chart lists the same three numbers, in the same order. Learn them once and every bearing chart reads the same.

The bore is the hole in the middle — the diameter of the shaft the bearing sits on. It is the first number to fix, because the shaft is already made and cannot be changed. Measure the shaft, or read it off the old bearing's part number, and find that bore in the left column of the chart.

The outside diameter is the outer ring — the size of the hole the bearing sits in. It is fixed by the housing, which is also already made. Read across from your bore to the outside diameter that fits your housing.

The width is the thickness of the bearing along the shaft. For a tapered roller bearing the width is measured across the whole assembled pair — cone plus cup together — not across one part alone. That is the number beginners get wrong: they measure the cone, order by that width, and the bearing arrives too narrow for the housing. Measure the assembled pair, or read the width off the chart for the matched pair.

3. Reading the chart in order

Start with the bore. Find your shaft size in the left column and stay on that row. Then read across to the outside diameter your housing takes. If two sizes share your bore and your outside diameter, the width decides: a wider bearing in the same bore and outside diameter carries more load, and a narrower one fits where space is tight. The chart lists them side by side so you can compare without guessing.

Next check the series. The series is the family the size belongs to — a light series, a medium series, a heavy series — and it tells you roughly how much load the bearing is built for in that size. A bigger series number in the same bore means a heavier bearing for a harder job. Do not pick a light-series bearing for a heavy job to save money: it will fail early, and the failure will cost more than the saving.

Then check the version: open or sealed, single row or double row. Most tapered roller bearings are supplied open, without seals, because they run in a housing that is sealed separately — in a gearbox, for example. If your bearing runs exposed to dust or water, ask about a sealed version at inquiry rather than assuming one exists for your size.

4. Single row or matched pair

One tapered roller bearing handles push from one side only. A shaft that is pushed from both sides — a wheel hub, for example — needs two of them, set facing opposite ways, so each one takes the push from its own side. The chart lists single-row sizes; the pair is your ordering decision, not a separate chart entry.

Order the pair as a matched set when the job needs it. A matched pair is made and measured together, so the two bearings share the load evenly. Two single bearings bought separately can work, but they may not share the load the same way, and the shaft will tell you about it in vibration and heat. For a wheel hub or a gearbox shaft, ask for the matched pair at inquiry — it costs little more and removes a whole class of problems.

If you are replacing one bearing in an old pair, replace both. A new bearing running against a worn partner carries more than its share, and the new one fails early. The pair wears together and should be replaced together.

5. What to send at inquiry

The fastest inquiry is the one that needs no reply to clarify. Send four things: the old bearing's part number if you have it, the three dimensions (bore, outside diameter, width) if you measured them, the machine and what it does, and the quantity. With those four, the size can be confirmed from the chart without a second round of questions.

If the part number is gone and you measured the bearing yourself, say so — and say how you measured it. A bore measured with a ruler is not the same as a bore measured with a caliper, and the supplier needs to know which one you used. Our how-to-measure-a-bearing guide explains the measuring steps in full.

Minimum order is 50 pieces. We supply small and medium bearings only, with an outside diameter up to 600 mm — larger sizes are outside what we make, so do not ask for them. Prices, delivery times and exact specifications are confirmed at inquiry: send the size and the application, and the quotation comes back with the size confirmed against the chart, not guessed.

FAQ

Can a tapered roller bearing replace a deep groove ball bearing? Only if the housing and the shaft suit it. The tapered bearing is wider in the same bore and handles side push that a ball bearing cannot. Check the housing width and the load direction first — the two types are not drop-in replacements for each other.

Do I order the cone and the cup separately? No. Order them as the matched pair for the size. The cone and the cup are made to fit each other, and mixing sizes does not work.

What if my shaft size is not on the chart? Then the bearing is either a special size or the shaft has been modified. Send the three measured dimensions and the application at inquiry — a size that is not on the standard chart needs a separate check, not a guess from the nearest row.

Why does the same bore come in different widths? Because the same shaft can carry different loads in different machines. A wider bearing in the same bore carries more load. Pick the width by the load, not by the price.

How do I know I read the chart right? Check it backwards: take the size you picked, look up its bore, outside diameter and width, and measure the old bearing or the shaft and housing against those three numbers. If all three match, the size is right.
