How to Read a Bearing Number: Bore Code, Series and Suffixes

Published: 2026-11-06 　|　 Industry News

A bearing number is a short code, not a random string. Read it in three parts: the last two digits usually give the bore size, the digits before them name the type and size series, and letters at the end describe seals, shields and clearance. Read the three parts in that order, then confirm the full designation with the supplier before ordering — because suffix letters differ from one maker to another, and a number copied wrong is a bearing that does not fit.

1. The three parts of a number

Every standard bearing designation has the same shape, even when the numbers look different at first glance.

PartWhere it sitsWhat it tells you
Bore codeLast two digitsThe bore diameter — the hole the shaft goes through
Type and series digitsBefore the bore codeThe bearing type and its width and diameter series
Suffix lettersAfter the digitsSeals, shields, clearance, precision and cage details

Take a familiar example shape, 6204. Reading right to left: the last two digits, 04, are the bore code; the digit before them, 2, is part of the series; the first digit, 6, names the type. A suffix such as ZZ or RS would sit after the digits and describe the sealing. That is the whole method: bore first, then type and series, then suffixes. The sections below explain each part.

2. The bore code: the last two digits

For most bearings with a bore of 20 mm and above, the bore code is simple: multiply the last two digits by 5, and the result is the bore diameter in millimetres. A bore code of 04 means a 20 mm bore, 05 means 25 mm, 08 means 40 mm, and 10 means 50 mm. This is the widely used designation system, and it is the fastest way to check whether a number matches the shaft you have.

Four small codes are exceptions, and they are worth memorising: 00 means a 10 mm bore, 01 means 12 mm, 02 means 15 mm, and 03 means 17 mm. Below 10 mm, and above the range where the multiply-by-5 rule applies, the bore is usually written directly in the designation instead — so if a number does not follow the rule above, do not force it. Measure the bore and send the measurement.

The bore code is the one part of a number you can usually trust at a glance. If the bore code says 40 mm and your shaft is 45 mm, the bearing will not fit, and no other part of the number can fix that. Check the bore first, before anything else.

3. Type and series digits: what kind of bearing it is

The digits before the bore code name the type and the size series. The first digit usually names the type, and the next digits name the width and diameter series — how wide and how bulky the bearing is for its bore.

The widely used type digits include: 6 for deep groove ball bearings, 7 for angular contact ball bearings, 3 for tapered roller bearings, and the roller families for cylindrical, spherical and thrust designs. On this site, the same families appear as product ranges: deep groove ball bearings for light and medium loads at higher speeds, cylindrical roller bearings for heavy radial loads where rigidity matters, and tapered roller bearings for combined radial and one-direction axial loads.

The series digits matter because two bearings with the same bore and the same type can still differ in width and outside diameter. A light series is narrower and smaller outside; a heavy series is wider and larger outside for the same bore. That is why a bore size alone never identifies a bearing: always read the series digits with it, and always confirm the outside diameter and width as well. In this small and medium range, outside diameter runs up to 600 mm, so a size that fits the shaft and housing without forcing or rework is the first practical test.

4. Suffixes: seals, shields, clearance and precision

Letters after the digits describe the details. The most common ones are worth knowing, but they are also the part where makers differ most, so treat this section as a guide to the usual meanings, not as a promise for every brand.

  • Z or ZZ — shields. A metal shield on one side (Z) or both sides (ZZ) keeps larger dirt out and keeps grease in. Shields are not seals: fine dust and water can still get past them.
  • RS or 2RS — seals. A rubber seal on one side (RS) or both sides (2RS) seals better against dust and moisture than a shield, and suits dirty or damp surroundings.
  • C3 and similar — clearance. Clearance is the internal play between the rolling elements and the rings. A C3 marking means a larger-than-normal clearance, often chosen where the bearing runs hot or with a tight fit. Standard clearance has no marking in many systems. If the machine runs hot, uses a tight fit, or carries a heavy load, ask for a clearance recommendation for that exact combination instead of assuming a standard clearance suits every case.
  • P6, P5 and similar — precision class. Most general machinery runs well on a standard precision class. Spindles, high-speed motors and instruments needing low vibration and tight running accuracy may need a higher class, but higher precision helps only when the shaft, housing and assembly accuracy can match it.
  • Cage letters. A letter near the end of the designation sometimes names the cage material or design. Cage letters vary the most between makers, so never guess what one means — ask.

If a suffix does not match this list, do not assume it is a mistake. It may be a maker-specific code for a seal material, a lubricant, a matched pair, or a special execution. Copy the full designation exactly as printed on the old bearing or the drawing, and send it with the inquiry.

5. What a bearing number cannot tell you

A number identifies a bearing. It does not describe the machine it goes into. Four things always need a separate answer in the inquiry:

  • Load. Direction, size and character — steady, varying or shock-like. A number says nothing about what the bearing must carry.
  • Speed. Normal running speed, maximum speed, and whether the machine starts and stops often.
  • Environment. Dust, moisture, washdown, chemicals or high temperature. Sealing and lubrication must be chosen for the place where the bearing will actually work.
  • Fit and mounting. Shaft and housing fits, and whether the bearing is mounted by press, heat or slip fit.

For general background on bearing types and load directions, see the bearing types reference. The final selection should still be confirmed against the size, load case and working conditions in the inquiry.

6. Sending an inquiry, with or without a number

If you have the full designation, send it exactly as printed, plus the quantity and the destination. That is the fastest inquiry a supplier can answer.

If you have no number, send the three dimensions instead: bore diameter, outside diameter and width, all in millimetres, plus the bearing series if you know it and the application in one line. If you only have the old bearing, measure the three dimensions and send them. If a dimension is not measured yet, say so — write "to be confirmed at inquiry" for that line rather than guessing a number.

For wheel hub units and railway axlebox bearings, send the series and the application range and ask for the full model at inquiry. There is no single size table for vehicle- or axle-specific units, and a guessed fitment will be wrong. Do not send only a competitor brand name and ask for "the same": brand names do not fix dimensions, clearance or precision, and we do not sell under another brand's name.

A complete inquiry therefore looks like this, in one message: the full designation if known, or bore / outside diameter / width + series + application + precision as written on your drawing (or "to be confirmed at inquiry") + quantity + packing requirement + destination. Send it through the contact form, and the reply will confirm the series match, the quantity, the price and the lead time for that exact inquiry.

Frequently asked questions

Can I order by the number alone? Usually yes, if the number is complete and copied exactly, including suffixes. A number without its suffixes can still be the wrong bearing — a sealed version and an open version share the same digits and behave differently.

What if the number on the old bearing is worn off? Measure the bore, outside diameter and width in millimetres, note the type by shape, and send the measurements with the application. Do not guess a number from a worn marking.

Does the same number from different makers mean the same bearing? Largely yes for the digits — the bore, type and series follow the widely used system — but suffix letters and internal details can differ. Confirm the full designation, including seals, clearance and precision, at inquiry.

What if my bearing is over 600 mm outside diameter? This range covers small and medium bearings up to 600 mm outside diameter only. Larger sizes are not handled through this route — do not send an enquiry for them here; they will be turned away at the first check, not quoted.
